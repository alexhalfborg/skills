# Hook craft

The brand-agnostic method `write-hook` drafts and ranks against. It carries no brand facts: voice,
claims and phrases to avoid come from the brand's own files.

A hook has one job: earn the next second of attention from the person the piece is for, without
promising anything the piece does not deliver. A hook that wins the click and loses the reader has
failed, and it costs the brand the next click as well.

## The angles

Draft across these, then keep the strongest of each angle that survives. Not every angle suits
every piece; a piece with no news in it has no news angle.

| Angle | What it does | Best when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Specific** | Leads with the piece's most concrete detail: a number, a name, an object, a moment | The piece has a detail nobody else would have | A number that is not in the piece, or rounded up |
| **Plain benefit** | Says what the reader gets, straight | The benefit is strong and easy to state | Going generic ("everything you need to know") |
| **Question** | Asks the thing the reader is already asking | The piece answers it, fully | A question the piece only half answers |
| **Reader situation** | Names a moment the reader recognises themselves in | The audience pain is sharp and common | Describing a situation instead of naming it |
| **News or timeliness** | Ties the piece to something happening now | There is a real date, release, or event | Manufactured urgency; "just in" when it is not |
| **Contrarian** | Pushes against a common belief | The piece makes that argument with evidence | Contrarian for its own sake; a straw man |
| **Story moment** | Opens on a moment from a person's experience | The piece is built on a real story | A moment the piece does not contain |
| **Voice tell** | Uses the brand's own turn of phrase | The voice profile has real tells | Forcing a tell where it does not fit |

## Per surface

Length limits below are approximate. Platforms change their cut-offs and they vary by device, so
treat each one as a guide, front-load the words that carry the meaning, and never present a figure
here to the user as a platform fact.

### Email: subject line and preview text

- The subject line and the preview text are **one unit**. Every option is a pair.
- Subject: aim for about 40 characters, so it survives a phone inbox. Put the meaning in the first
  few words.
- Preview text: completes or complicates the subject, never repeats it. Roughly a line's worth.
  A blank preview shows the first line of the body or raw markup, so it is never optional.
- Match the byline's register: a warm, peer-to-peer brand does not send a hype subject line.
- No ALL CAPS, emoji kept to a minimum, and never a fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" (that is deception, not a
  hook).

### Article or blog headline

- Says what the reader gets, in words they would use themselves.
- For anything meant to be found by search, put the topic words early; around 60 characters fits most
  search results before they cut off.
- A number headline ("7 ways…") only when the piece is a list of exactly that many.
- A question headline only when the piece answers it.
- No pun that hides the topic. Clever is fine; obscure is not.

### Social post: the first line

- The line before the platform's "more" cut-off, which is often only the first line or two on a
  phone. It must make sense entirely on its own.
- No hashtags and no emoji run in it; they spend the only space that is visible.
- Avoid opening on the brand's name. Open on the reader or the specific.

### Video (long form): title

- Front-load: titles are often cut off at around 60 to 70 characters in lists and on phones.
- Include the topic word someone would search for.
- The title and the thumbnail work as a pair; the title should not repeat the thumbnail's text. This
  skill does not write the thumbnail, but say so if the content suggests the thumbnail should carry
  something the title then leaves out.

### Short: one line for the first frame and the title

- One line that works both as text on the first frame and as the platform title.
- Readable in about a second: about six words, rarely more than 40 characters.
- Works with the sound off.
- Adds to the spoken opening rather than repeating it. If the first spoken line is "I tried this for
  a month", the frame line is not "I tried this for a month".
- No hashtags in the line.
- The words only. Where it sits on the frame (safe areas, crops, type) is decided when it is set onto
  the picture or burned into the clip, not here.

## Ranking order

The payoff test is a pass or fail filter, not a criterion: an option that fails it is not ranked at
all. Among the survivors, rank on these, in this order:

1. **Clear.** The reader knows what they get from one read.
2. **Specific.** It carries a concrete detail rather than a general claim.
3. **Relevant.** It speaks to a pain or job the audience actually has.
4. **Pulls.** There is a reason to keep going: tension, a gap, a turn. Earned, not withheld.
5. **On voice.** It sounds like the brand and the byline.
6. **Fits.** It sits comfortably inside the surface's length.

Ties go to the option that is clearer.

## The clickbait test

Run every option through these questions. A "no" to the first one, or a "yes" to any of the others,
fails it.

1. Would a reader who acted on this feel the piece kept the promise?
2. Does it imply a result, a claim, or a fact the piece does not contain, or that the brand has not
   cleared?
3. Is any urgency in it manufactured (a deadline, a scarcity, a "last chance" that is not real)?
4. Does it hide the topic entirely, so the reader cannot tell what they are clicking into?
5. Does it impersonate something it is not (a reply, a personal message, a system notice)?
6. Does it shout (ALL CAPS, stacked punctuation, an emoji wall)?

An option that fails only the first question, because the piece stops short of it, belongs under
**Stronger, not yet earned** with what the piece would need to add. Every other failure is dropped.

## Distinct, not reworded

Two options are the same option when they share an angle and make the same promise, however
differently they are worded. Keep the stronger one. Three distinct, honest options are worth more
than five where two are the same line twice.
